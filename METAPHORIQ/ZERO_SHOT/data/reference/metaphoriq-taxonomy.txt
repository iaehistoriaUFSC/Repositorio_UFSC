# 1 Metaphors for History  

## 1.1 Mirrors  
- “[…] the mirror of history has been associated with an image no less full of meaning: that of the naked truth.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 23).  
- “[The history is] the mirror of the past, guide of the present, lighthouse of the future”   
  Fonte: Ferrer del Río, Antonio (1850) Decadencia de España (Madrid: F. P. Mellado), p. XX.  
- “[The] history is the mirror in which the past reflects the future.”   
  Fonte: Tomás de Iriarte, 1858. In. Diccionario político y social del mundo iberoamericano – I, Fernández-Sebastián, ed. 2009, p. 585.; General Tomás de Iriarte (1858).  
- “He [historian] has to make of his brain a mirror, unclouded, bright, and true of surface; then he will reflect events as they presented themselves to him, neither distorted, discoloured, nor variable.”   
  Fonte: (Lucian of Samosata. The Way to Write History, § 51. Translated by H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler. Oxford: The Clarendon Press, 1905.).  
- “The history of England serves as a mirror to show me our future.”   
  Fonte: Stendhal, Le rouge et le noir, II, 1.; .  
- “If one looks upon history as a text, then one can say of it . . . that the past has left in them images comparable to those registered by a light-sensitive plate. The future alone possesses developers strong enough to reveal the image in all its details.”   
  Fonte: Benjamin, Walter. Selected Writings. v. 4. Cambridge, MA: The Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, p. 405.  
- “[is possible] to write history as if it were a sequence resulting from film editing, in which there is a close-up, a long shot, and so on”   
  Fonte: Ginzburg, Carlo (2014) “Historia y microhistoria. Carlo Ginzburg entrevistado por Mauro Boarelli”, Pasajes 44, p. 92.  
- “In the age of flm, the band of historical time could turn into a flmstrip that could rewind itself in a loop”  
  Fonte: Wimmer, Mario (2015) “The Present as Future Past: Anonymous History of Historical Times”, Storia della Storiograia 68(2), p. 183.  
- “Instead of supposing a single mirror reflecting happenings in an exterior world at the moment of their occurrence, it would be better to suppose [history as] a system of mirrors facing inward and outward at different angles, so that they reflect occurrences in the mirrored world largely through the diverse ways in which they reflect one another.”   
  Fonte: Pocock. J. G. A. Virtue, Commerce, and History. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1985, p. 29.  
- “The metaphors of picturing, mirroring, copying, photographing, reflecting – all expressions of the act of representing, and ones that have dominated the philosophy of history – become not just beside the point but indeed misleading with respect to understanding language in general or historiography in particular. More apt metaphors become those of moving a piece in a chess game, or discovering a solution to a puzzle, or diagnosing an illness, or arguing in a court of law – metaphors for moves in an ongoing, rule-governed practice that embodies certain basic ethical and ontological assumptions.”   
  Fonte: Fay, Brian (2016) “From Narrativism to Pragmatism”, Journal of the Philosophy of History 11 (1), p. 5.  

## 1.2 Perspectives  
- “[history is] a multifaceted column, [which should be examined] “on all its sides”.   
  Fonte: (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 31).; Alexandre Herculano (1842). In: Fernández-Sebastián, Javier, ed. (2009) Diccionario político y social del mundo iberoamericano. La era de las revoluciones, 1750-1850 (Madrid: CEPC), vol. I, p. 672.  
- “The range of vision that includes everything that can be seen from a particular vantage point.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 31).  
  Fonte: Gadamer (1960 / 2004).  
- “many historical sources might be described as ‘snapshots’, taken from a certain viewpoint at a certain moment.”   
  Fonte: Burke, Peter (2005) “Performing History: The Importance of Occasions”, Rethinking History 9(I), p. 47.  

## 1.3 Constructions  
- “the his torian’s business is not to discover, to recapture, or even to interpret; it is to create and to construct.”   
  Fonte: (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 32).; Oakeshott, Michael (1933). Experience and Its Modes. (Cambridge: Cam bridge University Press), p. 94-95.  

## 1.4 Masters and Teachers  
- “What experience and history teach is this – that nations and governments have never learned anything from history, or acted upon any lessons they might have drawn from it.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 32).  
  Fonte: Hegel (1830). Lectures on the Philosophy of World History .  
- “History as the mother of truth, ‘rival of time, storehouse of deeds, witness for the past, example and counsel for the present, and warning for the future’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 34).  
  Fonte: Cervantes (1605), Don Quixote, I, p. 9.  
- "[This work – the history- is] a possession for all time, not a prize composition to be heard for the moment." "[τόδε τὸ ἔργον ἐστὶ] κτῆμά τε ἐς αἰεὶ μᾶλλον ἢ ἀγώνισμα ἐς τὸ παραχρῆμα ἀκούειν ξύγκειται."    
  Fonte: Thucydides, Peloponnesian War, I, 1 and 22.  
- “the soundest education and training for a life of active politics is the study of History, and that the surest and indeed the only method of learning how to bear bravely the vicissitudes of fortune, is to recall the calamities of others.”   
  Fonte: Polybius, Historiae, I, 1. trad. Paton/Shuckburgh.  
- “History is truly the witness of times past, the light of truth, the life of memory, the teacher of life, the messenger of antiquity”. [“Historia vero est testis temporum, lux veritatis, vita memoriae, magistra vitae, nuntia vetustatis”]   
  Fonte: Cicero (55 BC), De oratore, II, 9, p. 36.  
- “history was past politics and politics present history”   
  Fonte: Seely, John apud Hunt, Lynn (2018) History. Why It Matters (Cambridge: Polity Press). p. 85.  
- “The statesman is the historian in practice.”   
  Fonte: Droysen (1897), § 93, p. 56.  
- “[history] teaches more in one day than others learnt from the experience of many years”.   
  Fonte: Juan de Santa María (1615), Tratado de república.  
- "the reading of history makes children mature, while ignorance thereof turns adults into little children". [“Historia si adsit ex pueris facit senes: sin absit, ex senibus pueros”]    
  Fonte: Vives, Juan Luis. (1555) De tradendis disciplinis [Concerning the Teaching of Disciplines].  
- “history is the master of true politics, and who best teaches the prince how to reign, because it incorporates the experience of all past governments and the prudence and wisdom of our ancestors”.   
  Fonte: Fajardo, Saavedra (1640). Political Maxims. Idea of a Christian Political Prince, IV.  
- “[history is] queen of the times […], teacher of life, life of fame, fame of truth, and truth of facts.”   
  Fonte: Gracián, Baltasar (1653). El Criticón [Book of Critique], II, p. 4.  
- “history is a school teacher who teaches modern societies to read the book in which they learn to spell ancient ones”   
  Fonte: Espinosa, Juan (1855). Diccionario para el Pueblo. In: Fernández-Sebastián, Javier, ed. (2009) Diccionario político y social del mundo iberoamericano. La era de las revoluciones, 1750-1850 (Madrid: CEPC), vol. I, p. 656.  
- “as the past has ceased to throw its light upon the future, the mind of man wanders in obscurity”.   
  Fonte: Tocqueville (1840). Democracy in America II, IV, p. 8.  
- “We no longer go to history for lessons in morals, nor for good examples of conduct, nor yet for dramatic or picturesque scenes. We understand that for all these purposes legend would be preferable to history, for it presents a chain of causes and effects more in accordance with our ideas of justice, more perfect and heroic characters, finer and more affecting scenes.”   
  Fonte: Ch.-V. Langlois & Ch. Seignobos, Introduction to the Study of History, trad. G. G. Berry (Londres/Nova York: Duckworth / Henry Holt & Co., 1898), p. 331.  
- “those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it”   
  Fonte: Santayana, George (1905). The Life of Reason.  

## 1.5 Clio Transfigured  
- “The task of history, once the other-world of truth has vanished, is to establish the truth of this world.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 37).  
  Fonte: Marx, *Crítica da Filosofia do Direito de Hegel* (1844).  

### 1.5.1 Railways and Trains  
- “The train of time, the railway of progress, the locomotive of history – three slightly different versions of the same imagery – travel purposefully forward at great speed, as they have to make up for lost time and arrive as soon as possible at their destination.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 38).  
- “History is about the most cruel of all goddesses, and she leads her triumphal car over heaps of corpses, not only in war, but also in ‘peaceful’ economic development.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 38).  
  Fonte: Engels (late 19th c; Carr 1990, 81).  
- “Maybe the revolution was not actually the locomotive, as Marx maintained, but rather its antithesis: the emergency brake that avoids a catastrophe.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 39).  
  Fonte: Benjamin (1927–1930 / 2006, 402).  

### 1.5.2 History in Motion  
- “Prominent among the images applied by its critics to the philosophy of history as such is that of the centaur … This pejorative metaphor emphasizes the monstrous nature of this strange creature, half man, half horse.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 40).  
  Fonte: Jacob Burckhardt, *Weltgeschichtliche Betrachtungen* (1905).  
- “The ‘world-soul on horseback’ that Hegel thought he saw going down the street in front of his house in Jena is easier to imagine … The Fourth Estate … revolutions of the 20th century in Mexico, Russia, or China.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 40).  
  Fonte: Rosenstone (2002, 479).  
- “Post-revolutionary historiography and romantic painting … were full of spirited stallions, smoking trains, monuments, ruins of the past, masses in movement, and revolutionaries in action.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 40).  
- “A painting by John Gast, *American Progress*, shows how the expansion brought the light of civilization … telegraph wire and a school book … railway line that enabled communication and transportation.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 41).  
- “All of them – texts and images – may be seen as historical-political symbols … graphic metaphors of History in action.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 42).  

### 1.5.3 Court of Justice and Dustbin  
- “The History of the World is the World’s Court of Justice.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 42).  
  Fonte: Löwith (1949, 12); Rosen (2014, 270); Koselleck (2004a, 60–65).  
- “My name already belongs to history: she will judge me.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 43).  
  Fonte: Simón Bolívar (1829; Aguilar 2000, 196).  
- “History will absolve me!” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 44).  
  Fonte: Fidel Castro, speech of 1953 (*Dela Cova 2007*).  
- “History will judge me, but just to be safe, I shall choose the historians.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 45).  
  Fonte: Andrés Rábago, *El País*, 11 Sept 2007.  

## 1.6 Counter-Metaphors  
- “Life that must judge history.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 46).  
  Fonte: Nietzsche, *The Use and Abuse of History for Life* (1874).  
- “The Angel of History denounces the outrage of progress and repudiates the glories once sung by the muse, which for the angel have been reduced to a heap of rubble.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 50).  
  Fonte: Walter Benjamin (1940).  

## 1.7 Turns, Levels, Professions, Territories, Borders  
- “It should be borne in mind that for centuries no clear borders existed between history and philosophy, rhetoric, theology, morality, geography, philology, literature, or politics – domains which today seem clearly distinguishable were not so then.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 53).  
- “The brightest star in the social firmament … a sphere that partly directs other activities and ‘lacks natural borders’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 53).  
  Fonte: René Rémond (1988, 380–385).

# 2 Time and Memory  

## 2.1 Memory of Time / Time of Memory  
- “Phrases such as ‘the wind blows,’ ‘the river flows,’ or ‘time passes’ seem to suggest that the wind, the river, or time was something substantially different from their movement — the act of blowing, flowing, or passing — which is their essential distinguishing trait.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 62).  
  Fonte: Norbert Elias (1993) *Über die Zeit* (1984).  

## 2.2 Clio, Chronos, and Kairos  
- Clio is often shown writing on Chronos’ back (17th–18th c.) (Fernández-Sebastián 2021c; SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 63).  
  Fonte: *Bibliothecae Alexandrinae icones symbolicae* (Cristoforo Giarda, 1628).  
- The destructive power of Chronos (“time the devourer”) contrasts with Clio’s preserving mission. Herodotus wrote that he aimed “that time does not abolish men’s achievements and to ensure that their great exploits do not fade into oblivion.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 63).  
  Fonte: *Histories* (Herodotus, 5th c. BC).  
- Sculpture in Wiblingen Abbey shows Clio halting Chronos as he tries to tear out a page of history, symbolizing the struggle between memory and time (Ricoeur 2004).  
  Fonte: Paul Ricoeur (2004).  
- “Time the destroyer is time the preserver.” (T. S. Eliot, *Four Quartets*).  
- “Time is the school in which we learn, / Time is the fire in which we burn.” (Delmore Schwartz, *For Rhoda*).  
- A baroque engraving by Jesuit Giambattista Riccioli (*Chronologia*, 1669) shows Clio’s subordination to chronology and astronomy. Kant later called “for chronology to follow the lead of history instead of the reverse,” anticipating ideas of “history without chronology.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 64).  
  Fonte: Riccioli (1669); Kant in Koselleck 2018; Tanaka 2019.  
- “The time is fulfilled.” (*Mark 1:15*).  
- “The fullness of time.” (*Gal. 4:4*).  
  Fonte: Cullmann, *Christus und die Zeit* (1946).  
- “The time is ripe for a revolution.” — Tariq Ali on May 1968 in France. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 64).  

## 2.3 Circles, Lines, and Points  
- Classical cyclical vs. Christian linear time (Augustine, *City of God*). (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 64).  
- Eternity as non-time; Gnostic interruptions; messianic expectations; spiral time (Latour 1993; SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 65).  
- Indigenous Colombian spiral conception (Rappaport 2003).  
- Spanish Jesuit Nieremberg’s *Treatise on the Difference Between the Temporal and the Eternal* (1640).  
- Revolution as “a tiger’s leap into the past.” (Benjamin 1940/2007, p. 261).  

## 2.4 Water, Rivers, and Seas  
- The river as one of the oldest metaphors of time — fluid yet continuous — recalling Heraclitus. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 67).  
  Fonte: Heraclitus (c. 500 BC).  
- “All social reality came to be seen as a historical stream or river.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 67).  
  Fonte: Tamm and Olivier (2019, 4).  
- “The story is our only boat for sailing on the river of time.” — Ursula K. Le Guin, *A Fisherman of the Inland Sea* (1994).  
- Ricoeur: narrative as the “guardian of time.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 67).  
- Bismarck called himself the “boatman on the river of time.” He wrote: “man can neither create nor direct the stream of time, he can only travel upon it and steer with more or less skill.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 69).  
  Fonte: Clark (2019, 118–130).  
- Chateaubriand and Tocqueville likened nations to rivers with unreturnable sources; Husserl described the “primal source-point” feeding time consciousness (Ricoeur 2004a).  
- Real Academia de la Historia (1738) emblem: a fountain with motto “In patriam popvlvmque flvit” symbolizing “the river of time.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 69).  
- “Language is the ocean in which we all swim.” (Kelley 2002, 300). Gadamer used the sea to explain the historicity of understanding.  
- “He who serves a revolution ploughs the sea.” — Simón Bolívar (1830).  
- 20th-century thinkers (Heidegger, Canguilhem, Serres) criticized the linear-river metaphor, proposing viscous, opaque, or turbulent temporalities. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 71).  

## 2.5 Atmospheres and Thresholds  
- “History, with the Annales, flowed out of the river of time and settled into an atmospheric inertia.” (Marlon Salomon 2018a, 149).  
- Febvre viewed the 16th-century Christian world as an “atmosphere” enveloping all — not merely a set of beliefs. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 72–73).  
- Expressions like *genius saeculi*, *Zeitgeist*, and “spirit of the age” prefigured the metaphor of atmosphere. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 73).  
- “Democracy is the principle of these times… present in the atmosphere we live in, in the air we breathe.” — Ordax Avecilla, *Diario de Sesiones*, 16 Dec 1854.  
- Stefan Zweig, *The World of Yesterday* (1943), contrasted factual history with the difficulty of reconstructing its “spiritual atmosphere.”  

## 2.6 Levels, Sediments, Strata  
- Unamuno compared daily life to the “sea of history,” where surface events are waves above deep intrahistory. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 75).  
- “The almost motionless seabed constitutes true, universal, and everlasting tradition.” (*En torno al casticismo*, 1895).  
- Braudel distinguished surface “evental” waves from deep structural time, slow as nature’s rhythms. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 75).  
- Löwith likened surface turmoil to waves over a silent sea-bed. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 75).  

## 2.7 Turbulent Times  
- “Turbulence brings us back to the metaphorics of flow… furious currents that drag us to unknown destinations.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 78).  
- “It is like the glass front of a washing machine…” (Koselleck 2004b, 260).  
- “Time was splintered… shards cutting into your mind.” — Ruth Kluger, *Weiter leben* (1992); *Still Alive* (2001).  
- “The creator of allegories works with shards.” (Kittsteiner 1996, 61).  
- Postmodern narrative evokes “frayed time.” (Brantly 1997, 310–11).  
- Leonardo Padura, *La transparencia del tiempo* (2018): superimposed historical moments contradicting opaque “mists of time.”  
- Metaphors of palimpsests, mosaics, and folds express fragmented temporalities.  
- “Time is written into the strata of the planet.” (Chakrabarty 2021, 171).  

## 2.8 A Deluge of Memory  
- “Over the course of the 1980s, an initial trickle of writing on history and memory became a flood.” (Megill 1998, 43).  
- “When we first conceived cultural memory… it was then a small river; today, it has grown into a sea.” (Assmann 2011, xi).  
- “The sites of memory are like shells left on the shore when the sea of living memory has receded.” (Hartog 2015, 126).  
- Plato’s metaphors: the wax tablet (imprint) and the aviary (storage of thoughts) initiated durable memory imagery. (*Phaedrus*; *Theaetetus*).  
- “For true memory, inscription is a kind of sowing.” (Ricoeur 2004a, 143).  
- Geological metaphors (strata, sediment, excavation) link time and memory. (Warren 2015; Barash 2016).  
- Koselleck’s “sluices of memory” describe dramatic transitions from solid sediments to fluid recollection after historical shocks. (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 89).

# 3 Pasts, Presents, and Futures  

## 3.1 The Essential Triad  
- “Every historic present involves three distinct times, three different ‘todays’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 99).  
  Fonte: Ortega y Gasset (1962, 42–43).  
- “The past is a previous future and a recent present, the present is an impending past and a recent future, and finally, the future is a present and even a past to come.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 99).  
  Fonte: Merleau-Ponty (2012, 445).  
- “A certain romantic view of history envisioned Europe as the present, inheritor of a past that was connected to Asia, and seeking young America as a reflection of its future.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 99).  
  Fonte: Duque (1997, 132).  

## 3.2 Past and Present  
- “The character of the past is that it connects what is unconnected in the merging of one present into another.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 105).  
  Fonte: George Herbert Mead (1929).  
- “History is about the past for the sake of the present.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 105).  
  Fonte: Nikulin (2017, 174).  
- “Ortega y Gasset insisted time and time again that the past and the future only exist in the present. The present would be the knot that ties in a bundle the other two dimensions of time, in such a way that history would be ‘a science of the present’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 105).  
  Fonte: Ortega y Gasset (1961, 83, 212–13, 223; 1962, 198–199; Safranski 2015, 227).  

### 3.2.1 A Foreign Country  
- “The past is a foreign country, and, as Hartley wrote, ‘they do things differently there.’” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 107).  
  Fonte: L. P. Hartley, *The Go-Between* (1953).  
- “In this metaphor, of which there are several variants, lies the gist of the modern idea of history, which acknowledges the existence of a deep cut between the present and the past and at the same time constantly strives to suture it.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 107).  

### 3.2.2 The Living and the Dead  
- “History is a tomb for the corpse past, insofar as it allows society to draw a line between what is dead and what is not, calming the dead who still haunt the present by offering them scriptural tombs.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 109).  
  Fonte: Michel de Certeau (1988, viii, 2, 46–47).  
- “Instead of the metaphor of past-dead, she recommends that the past should continue living in the present, albeit in ghostly form.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 110).  
  Fonte: Victoria Fareld (2016).  
- “Far beneath its surface, the struggles of history, long dead, worked their powerful chemistry: here were the corpses of feudalism and absolutism, in various stages of decay; here were the ashes of heretics, the blood of rebels, the nourishing mineral relics of ignorance and patriotism. There was scarcely an institution, political or social, which did not flourish in this earth and grow fat.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 111).  
  Fonte: George Dangerfield, *The Strange Death of Liberal England* (1935; 2012, 25).  

### 3.2.3 Distances and Dimensions  
- “The real succession of events descends from the origin down to their most recent ordering; the universal historian ascends from the most recent world situation upwards toward the origin of things.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 111).  
  Fonte: Friedrich Schiller, *What Is, and to What End Do We Study Universal History* (1789).  
- “We understand then that the historian’s ‘secret weapon’ is a double-edged sword. Retrospectiveness is certainly an advantage, but it involves the risk of misinterpreting past events and incurring in that kind of presentist arrogance that E. P. Thompson memorably labelled ‘the enormous condescension of posterity’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 112).  

## 3.3 Present and Future  
- “Past events are contained in our experience and are empirically verifiable. What is to come is fundamentally beyond our experience and, as such, is not empirically verifiable.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 112).  
  Fonte: Hobbes, *Leviathan* (I, iii); Koselleck (2002, 132).  
- “A permanently open window in a moving vehicle or a watchtower on a mountain-top, but also that of the eye of the hurricane, that of a kind of blind spot from which you cannot see your own eyeball, that of the eye of a needle through which the thread of time passes, but also that of a trap, tie, or prison, as when it is said that, just as nobody can jump upon their own shadow, one is inescapably anchored in the present.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 115).  
  Fonte: Safranski (2015, 327).  

### 3.3.1 Lights  
- “The past is like a lamp placed at the entrance to the future, to dispel some of the darkness that covers it.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 120).  
  Fonte: Lamennais (1825).  
- “The past had ceased to throw its light upon the future, and consequently the mind of man wandered in obscurity.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 120).  
  Fonte: Tocqueville, *Democracy in America* (1840).  
- “It is highly symptomatic in this respect that the future is sometimes metaphorized as a dead star – the guiding star of enlightened and positivist modernity would have ceased to shine.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 120).  
  Fonte: Ruiz (2022, 11–12).  

### 3.3.2 Seeds  
- “Every century carries in it the seed of the events that will unfold in the ensuing century.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 121).  
  Fonte: Bernardo de Monteagudo, *Sobre la necesidad de una Federación General entre los Estados Hispano-Americanos y plan de su organización* (Lima, 1825).  
- “Our Humanity is only preparation, the bud of a future flower.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 122).  
  Fonte: Herder, *Outlines of a Philosophy of the History of Man* (1800).  
- “Under corruption, future good will germinate, as good seed germinates under manure.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 123).  
  Fonte: Francisco Pi y Margall, *La reacción y la revolución* (1854).  

### 3.3.3 Horizons  
- “Every living thing can become healthy, strong, and fruitful only within a horizon.”   
  Fonte: Friedrich Nietzsche (On the Advantage and Disadvantage of History for Life, 1874, § 1 and 9).  

### 3.3.4 Shards  
- “The death of the future.”  
  Fonte: Hartog 2003.  
- “The irruption of too much future at once, like a wave that breaks and sweeps the deck of a ship struggling in heavy weather.”  
  Fonte: Hartog 2020, 331.

# 4 Sources, Events, Processes  

## 4.1 Sources and Traces  
-   
- “For instance, historians supposedly drink or feed from historical sources — sources that, as the case may be, might be abundant and inexhaustible but also insufficient, drying up, and disappearing. Yet, above all, it is supposed that whoever is supplied by uncontaminated and crystalline sources — that is, whoever has original and reliable evidence — will almost automatically produce undisputed masterpieces, as if the purity of the sources guaranteed the veracity of the assertions deriving from them.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 136).  
  Fonte: Fernández Sebastián.  
- “The primitive ‘source’ does not consist in the dreary maze of contemporary opinions, accounts, reports. This is only the daily repeated atmospheric process of ascending and self-precipitating vapors from which the true sources or springs are replenished.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 137).  
  Fonte: Johann Gustav Droysen (1897, §34, 24).  
- “The memory of the world is not a bright, shining crystal, but a heap of broken fragments, a few fine flashes of light that break through the darkness.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 137).  
  Fonte: David Lowenthal (2015, 337).  

## 4.2 Events and Facts  
- “Events happen, facts are established.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 142).  
  Fonte: Hayden White (2008, 13).  
- “An event would be a ‘black hole’ of meaning, as the Holocaust has been referred to.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 143).  
  Fonte: Jörn Rüsen (2008, 189).  
- “Surface disturbances, crests of foam that the tides of history carry on their strong backs.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 145).  
  Fonte: Fernand Braudel, *La Méditerranée* (1949 / 1972).  

## 4.3 Processes and Structures  
- “A historian worthy of the name must represent every event as part of an entirety.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 150).  
  Fonte: Wilhelm von Humboldt (late 18th–early 19th c.).  
- “History [understood here as structure] is the keyboard on which these individual notes [i.e., the events] are sounded.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 152).  
  Fonte: Fernand Braudel (1949 / 1972, II, 1243).  
- “History then becomes a process in nature, a sequence of waves, a glacial drift, a tectonic fault movement, a flood, or an alluvial deposit.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 153).  
  Fonte: Hans Blumenberg (1985, 102).

# 5 Revolution, Crisis, Modernity  

## 5.1 Revolution  
- “We jumped aboard the carriage of the revolution, and dragged from precipice to precipice, in a few hours we completed the journey that should have taken many years.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 175).  
  Fonte: Flores (1853 / 1968, 18–19, 265; Fernández-Sebastián 2021c, 432–3).  
- “Sometimes a culture is in the greatest need of a brake: namely, when it is going too fast downhill or, as in the present case perhaps, too fast uphill.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 176).  
  Fonte: Friedrich Nietzsche (1878 / 1996, 378).  
- “Supra-gigantic forces whose movements race through humanity, dragging every individual willy-nilly with them – either riding atop their triumphant car or crushed under its wheels.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 184).  
  Fonte: Hannah Arendt (20th c; 2005, 341).  

## 5.2 Crisis  
- “We stand in the middle of the kind of great crisis that bridges the gap between one epoch and another, a crisis that resembles that of the Crusades, that of the era of Reformation, when America appeared on the horizon of history.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 187).  
  Fonte: Hans-Georg Gadamer (2004, 204–6, 211; Martin 2010, 316; echoes Schlegel 1810).  
- “All spiritual growth takes place by leaps and bounds, both in the individual and in the community. The crisis is to be regarded as a new nexus of growth.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 187).  
  Fonte: Jacob Burckhardt (1873 / 1943, 158).  
- “Moment of change which sheds light on all the elements of social formation, and all the levels of reality.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 190).  
  Fonte: Dhondt (1971, 57).  

## 5.3 Modernity  

### 5.3.1 Revelation, Discovery, Disappointment  
- “The historical reception of Copernicus’s theories and the subsequent cosmological discoveries deprived the universe of its metaphysical content, transformed it into an immense silent and indifferent galactic desert, and obliterated the cosmic importance that had traditionally been attributed to human beings and to their privileged home, planet Earth.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 194).  
  Fonte: Hans Blumenberg (1987).  

### 5.3.2 Action and Mastery  
- “Invisible hand.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 196).  
  Fonte: Adam Smith (18th c.).  
- “Ruse of nature.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 196).  
  Fonte: Immanuel Kant (18th c.).  
- “Cunning of Reason.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 196).  
  Fonte: Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (19th c.).  

### 5.3.3 Rationalization and Secularization  
- “Light of reason.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 196).  
  Fonte: René Descartes (17th c.).  
- “Man would be erased, like a face drawn in sand at the edge of the sea.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 197).  
  Fonte: Michel Foucault, *The Order of Things* (1966).  

### 5.3.4 A Perennial Gale  
- “Plethora of theories that printing casts on a daily basis into the maelstrom of systems shared by the modern world.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 199).  
  Fonte: Mariano José de Larra (1836).  
- “In order that any form of modernity may be worthy of becoming antiquity, the artist must be able to distil the eternal from the transitory.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 199).  
  Fonte: Henri Meschonnic (1988, 112–120).  

### 5.3.5 The Pile of Sand and the Contract  
- “In the absence of a common goal capable of engaging all the individuals of which it is comprised, society is no more than a pile of sand that the least jolt or the slightest puff will suffice to scatter.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 201).  
  Fonte: Émile Durkheim, *Moral Education* (1902–1903).  

### 5.3.6 Globalization and Great Acceleration  
- “Global finance, global empire, global apartheid, global terror, and global warming do not sound the same as global democracy, global trade, global ecosystem, global reduction in famine, and global migration. … Both the advocates of globalization and its detractors have a rich arsenal of images from which to choose and a growing specific vocabulary.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 203).  
  Fonte: Fernández Sebastián (2024, p. 203); Metaphorical expressions such as “Clash of Civilizations” (S. Huntington), “Jihad vs. McWorld” (B. Barber), “Tourists and Vagabonds” (Z. Bauman), and “Fences and Windows” (N. Klein).  
- “Among the metaphors that first sought to reflect this process is the famous and controversial expression ‘global village,’ referring to an emerging electronic age … Three decades later, sociologist Saskia Sassen would popularize the term ‘global city’ in a different sense.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 203).  
  Fonte: Marshall McLuhan, *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962); Saskia Sassen (1990s); Georg Simmel had already identified the metropolis and the rapid circulation of money as core metaphors for modernity (Rosa & Scheuerman 2009, 21).  
- “Talk of flows corresponds perfectly with a phase of modernity that has been described as liquid – for Bauman, fluidity is the key metaphor of the world today – and its logic can easily be combined with networks. … canal systems, roads, railways, telegraphs; anatomic and neuronal; geographical and fluvial; institutional and interpersonal; urban and commercial.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 204).  
  Fonte: Sternberger (1977, 24–7); Friedrich (2009, 2012); Gießmann (2016); Fernández-Sebastián (2021c, 206–9).  
- “Although the semantic core of this imaginary presents two facets – one biological (the spider’s web) and another technical (the fishing net) – both interacted so that in their evolution nature and culture fed off one another. … concepts such as network society and world wide web became common, understood as ‘the nervous system of the 21st century’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 204).  
  Fonte: Friedrich (2009, 293).  
- “Other in-vogue expressions such as ‘social networks,’ ‘the Connected Age,’ or ‘human web’ corroborate the extraordinary success of a metaphor that is no longer perceived as a metaphor but as a cardinal aspect of reality.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 204).
#### Acceleration  
- “Among the most consolidated signs of acceleration I would highlight, on the one hand, two geometrical figures – the exponential curve and the spiral – and, on the other, a notion taken from aeronautics: take-off. … It has been widely used to evidence the existence of a Great Acceleration or anthropogenic acceleration.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 205).  
  Fonte: Steffen et al. (2004); Bouton (2022, 300–8).  
- “Another ancestral semiotic figure that effectively captures the dynamics of modernity is the spiral … Rosa (2013) uses it to explain the dynamism of modern societies.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 205).  
  Fonte: Hartmut Rosa (2013).  
- “Hyper-acceleration has led in recent decades to a strange condition, a sort of ‘frenetic standstill’ (H. Rosa) or ‘frozen time’ (P. Virilio).” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 205).  
  Fonte: Hartmut Rosa; Paul Virilio.  
- “What prevails is more ‘a sense of directionless, frantic motion that is in fact a form of inertia’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 205).  
  Fonte: Rosa & Scheuerman (2009, 101).  
- “Another image frequently employed to describe acceleration … is take-off, popularized by W. W. Rostow more than six decades ago to clarify the crucial stage of economic development. … It is easy to see how the concept of take-off could be applied to the increasing acceleration of the modern world.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 206).  
  Fonte: W. W. Rostow (20th c.).  
- “Finally, acceleration has also been metaphorized and symbolized on occasions by recourse to certain types of clocks and instruments that measure time.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 206).  

### 5.3.7 Perverse Effects and Counter-Metaphors  
- “A runaway engine of enormous power which, collectively as human beings, we can drive to some extent but which also threatens to rush out of our control and which could rend itself asunder.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 207).  
  Fonte: Anthony Giddens (1991, 139); see also Hickel (2021, 106).  
- “However, the metaphor of modernity is no longer the ‘iron cage’ but the ‘gas chamber’.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 208).  
  Fonte: Gerard Delanty (2000, 52).  
- “Many use grim comparisons for (hyper)modernity, such as a shipwreck, a maelstrom, or a mega-machine … Others prefer melancholic metaphors such as ‘insatiable Moloch’ or ‘time bomb,’ while our ‘oscillating present’ is compared to ‘constant fibrillation.’ Some have even equated the rampant growth of our society with the metastasis of terminal cancer.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, pp. 208–209).  
  Fonte: Chignola (2020, 529).

# 6 Progress, Decline, Transition  

:::desc
All these terms derive from a handful of Latin verbs: *progredi* (to walk forward), *clinare/declinare* (to bend; to bend downward), *cadere/decadere* (to fall), *transire* (to go across), and *tradere* (to deliver, hand down). (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 219; cf. Nisbet 1969, 242–243, 271–272).
:::

## 6.1 Progress, Advancement, Development  
- “Just as the ivy on a tree cannot go higher than that on which it climbs and often grows downward after it has reached the top, the followers of Aristotle often end up with less understanding than their master.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 223).  
  Fonte: René Descartes (1637).  
- “Marx argued that, in the absence of a Socialist revolution, progress would resemble ‘that hideous pagan idol, who would not drink the nectar but from the skulls of the slain.’” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 224).  
  Fonte: Karl Marx, *New York Daily Tribune*, 8 August 1853.  

## 6.2 Decadence and Decline  
- “One might say that with the death of meaning in history, the idea of progress died too.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 238).  
  Fonte: Georg G. Iggers (1965, 5–10).  

## 6.3 Tradition and Transition  
- “Outside tradition there is no place for true originality; everything that is not tradition is plagiarism.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 241).  
  Fonte: Eugenio D’Ors.  
- “The human mind has moved from the absolute to the historical; it now considers everything under the category of becoming.” (SEBASTIÁN, 2024, p. 248).  
  Fonte: Ernest Renan (1890, 380).
